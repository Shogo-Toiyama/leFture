### WHAT MAKES AN IDEA GOOD (read carefully — most attempts fail here)

A weak idea describes a general category: "cloud providers use this technique," "game engines rely on this trick," "this is common in the finance industry." These are NOT acceptable. They are true, but boring, because they don't name anything a person could actually look up, verify, or be surprised by.

A strong idea names a specific, real, identifiable thing and tells you something about it you wouldn't already assume. It survives this test: if you swapped out the specific name for a generic category name and the sentence still basically makes sense, the idea is too weak.

Every candidate idea must include:
- **named_instance**: A specific, real, identifiable entity — a company, product, mission, system, historical event, person, or organization. NOT a category or industry (e.g., not "cloud providers," not "game engines," not "airlines" — but "AWS S3," "the Apollo Guidance Computer," "Falcon 9's landing algorithm," "Zara's supply chain").
- **concrete_detail**: A specific fact, number, mechanism, decision, constraint, or consequence tied to that named instance — something that couldn't be guessed just from knowing the general concept.
- **domain**: The real-world field this idea lives in (e.g., "orbital mechanics," "19th-century textile manufacturing," "professional esports," "supply chain logistics").

### SENSE FIDELITY RULE
`${CONCEPT_FOCUS}` may be a word or phrase that carries multiple unrelated meanings in different fields (e.g., "equality," "balance," "resolution," "inheritance"). Every candidate MUST connect to the SAME meaning described in `<CONCEPT_INTRO_LINE>` — not a different sense of the same word that happens to sound like a clever pun. Sharing a label is not sharing a concept. Before accepting any candidate, check: does the underlying mechanism/idea in `<CONCEPT_INTRO_LINE>` actually map onto this candidate, or does the connection only work because the words happen to match? If it's the latter, discard it no matter how compelling it sounds.

### TOPIC DISTANCE RULE (CRITICAL — replaces any notion of "avoid the lecture's field")
The goal here is NOT to run away from the lecture's academic field. A candidate in the same broad field as the lecture (e.g., History, in a History lecture) is completely fine and can be a great pick. The actual goal is: this must not feel like a continuation of today's specific topic — it should feel like an independent, standalone piece of trivia, not "more of the same class."

- **Discard** any candidate whose `named_instance` is essentially the same specific subject as `${TOPIC_TITLE}` / `<CONCEPT_INTRO_LINE>` (e.g., if today's topic was specifically about Arlington National Cemetery's headstones, a candidate that's just "more facts about Arlington National Cemetery" is a repeat wearing a costume — discard it).
- **Keep** any candidate that names a genuinely different specific subject, even if it sits in the same broad field or shares the same underlying meaning of the concept (e.g., a different specific memorial, event, or system that expresses the same concept in its own distinct way).
- **Do not guess about other courses.** You have no visibility into what else this student has learned in other classes, past or future. Do not try to infer or avoid "things they've probably already covered elsewhere" — you don't have that information, so don't act as if you do. Judge freshness only against `${TOPIC_TITLE}` / `<CONCEPT_INTRO_LINE>`, which is the one thing you actually know was just taught.
- A candidate from an unexpected field is still a nice bonus for surprise value (see Step 3), but it is a bonus, never a requirement.

Historical connections are just as welcome as present-day ones — a concept doesn't need to be "currently used somewhere" to count. What matters is specificity (named_instance + concrete_detail) and passing the two rules above, not recency or field-distance.

### PROCESS (do not skip steps, but do not show this work in your output)

**Step 1 — Diverge:** Generate 4 to 6 candidate connections between ${CONCEPT_FOCUS} and the real world, using the meaning established in `<CONCEPT_INTRO_LINE>`. Actively vary the type of connection across candidates — mix historical events, current products/companies, unexpected industries, cases where the concept's absence caused a failure, and, where relevant, something tied to the student's specific stated interests. For each, draft a `named_instance`, `concrete_detail`, and `domain`.

**Step 2 — Filter:** Discard any candidate that fails SENSE FIDELITY (relies on a different meaning of the word/phrase than `<CONCEPT_INTRO_LINE>` describes). Discard any candidate that fails TOPIC DISTANCE (is essentially the same specific subject as `${TOPIC_TITLE}`). Also discard any candidate where `named_instance` is actually just a category in disguise (an industry, a broad class of products, a vague "many companies do this"), or where `concrete_detail` could apply to almost anything in that category.

**Step 3 — Rank and select:** From the surviving candidates, pick the single strongest one by weighing:
- **Topical Fit:** How directly this connects to the student's stated major, interests, or goals in `<STUDENT_PROFILE>`.
- **Values/Style Fit:** If `<STUDENT_PROFILE>` contains explicit signals about what kinds of stories or angles excite this student — beyond just their stated major/interests (e.g., a stated love of cutting-edge technology, human/emotional stories, everyday phenomena, historical drama).
- **Surprise:** How much this breaks an assumption or reveals something non-obvious. Avoid repeating surface-level facts that everyone already knows.
- **Field Novelty (bonus, not a filter):** A candidate from a field distinct from the lecture's own can get a small edge here, since it reads as more unexpected — but never let this override a same-field candidate that already won on the criteria above.

**Step 4 — Output only the winner.** Do not output your other candidates or your reasoning — only the final selected idea.

### WRITING THE SEED
- `seed_fun_fact_idea`: 1–3 sentences, written as plain factual raw material — not a pitch, not a title, not a hook. State the named instance, the concrete detail, and (briefly) why it connects to ${CONCEPT_FOCUS}. Do not use enthusiastic marketing language, exclamation points, or emojis here — that styling happens in a later step. Do not use phrases like "this shows the student," "this will help their career," "this connects the lecture to," or any other sentence that explains the pedagogical value of the idea instead of just stating the idea itself.

### WEB SEARCH
This idea will later be written up in more detail using live web results, so your job is to figure out whether verification or added specificity is needed — not to broaden the topic.
- `needs_web_search`: true if the idea depends on a fact, number, mechanism, or story you are not fully certain of, or if more specific detail would meaningfully strengthen it. false only if the idea is already fully self-contained and verifiable from general knowledge (e.g., well-established math/history facts).
- `search_queries`: 2–3 queries, written as keyword-based web search queries — NOT full sentences or questions. Strip conversational phrasing entirely (no "how", "what is", "tell me about"); use only the essential names/terms a person would actually type into a search engine. These must dig deeper into the SAME named_instance and concrete_detail you already selected — do not use these queries to search for alternative ideas or broader industry context.
  - You may wrap an exact name, phrase, or error message in quotation marks (`"..."`) when precision matters — this forces an exact-match search instead of a loose keyword match.
  - Include a specific year or time period when it would help surface the right era of result (e.g. a historical event vs. a recent one) — avoid vague words like "recent" or "latest", which don't narrow a keyword search.
  - Favor specific, narrow phrasing (exact names, "engineering blog," "case study," "post-mortem," specific years) over generic terms.
- `search_reasoning`: One sentence on what specifically you need to confirm or enrich, and why it matters for this idea.
