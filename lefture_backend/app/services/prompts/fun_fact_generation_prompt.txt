### THE AHA TOOLKIT (choose 2–3 as your storytelling approach — for your own internal use only, never name these categories or their labels in your writing)
Use these as lenses to decide HOW to tell the story, not as phrases to include in it:
1. **Reality Connection:** This concept is quietly running something the student already interacts with.
2. **Future Self Connection:** This is a real, specific way it will matter in their actual future work — not a generic career platitude.
3. **The Cognitive Flip:** "You'd assume X. It's actually Y."
4. **The Secret Lens:** This one concept quietly links two things that seem unrelated.
5. **The Hidden Advantage:** Knowing this gives them a specific, describable edge — described in concrete terms, not labeled as an "edge."
6. **The Near-Disaster:** A real failure this concept could have prevented (or did cause).
7. **Narrative & Scene:** Tell it as a moment — a person, a decision, a scene — not a mechanism.
8. **The Memorable Punchline:** End on a specific, quotable line — grounded in the actual fact, not a generic slogan.

### CRITICAL WRITING RULES

**1. Ban the "Power Grant" Sentence Pattern (not just the words):**
Do not write any sentence whose structure is "this gives/grants/hands you a [power / edge / weapon / superpower / cheat code / unfair advantage]" — regardless of which specific noun fills in the blank. This applies to ALL synonyms and rephrasings of that same sentence shape, not just the words listed in this prompt. If you notice yourself writing a sentence that claims the student now possesses some abstract form of power or advantage, stop and rewrite it using one of the CONCRETE TITLE PATTERNS or a direct statement of what they'd actually be able to do, build, notice, or explain differently.

**2. CONCRETE TITLE / OPENING PATTERNS (use these instead):**
- **The Number:** lead with a specific, surprising figure. ("The 10GB Trick Behind Every Cloud Bill")
- **The Reversal:** state the assumption, then break it. ("Your Hard Drive Is Lying to You")
- **The Named Culprit:** put the specific entity in the title. ("How Sparse Files Quietly Run Netflix")
- **The Question:** pose the exact question the story answers. ("Why Did This Rocket Turn Sideways?")
- **The Confession:** frame it as something insiders know that outsiders don't. ("What Game Engines Don't Tell You")

**3. Zero Academic Re-Lecturing:**
The student already sat through the lecture. Do not re-explain `<CONCEPT_FOCUS>` beyond the single sentence in the `hook`. The entire `body` is dedicated to the specific, real-world story from the seed — not a refresher on the theory.

**4. The Hook Is Your Only Chance:**
The first 1–2 sentences must make the student instantly know what specific thing this story is about and sense why it's surprising. A vague tease ("you won't believe what this connects to...") is a failure — name the actual thing.
